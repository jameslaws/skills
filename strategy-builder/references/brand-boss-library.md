## 1. Positioning: what you stand for

### 1.1 Stand for one thing
**Rule:** A brand can stand for only one thing: Volvo safety, Porsche performance, Mercedes luxury. Pick the one thing you do best, enjoy, and make the most money doing. Then make every message, sales deck and product ladder up to it.
**Why:** People remember one thing about a brand. "If you're trying to be everything to everybody, you're going to be memorable to no one." A buffet has everything, but "no one ever goes to a buffet restaurant to get a great steak." The specialist is trusted more and can charge more. Brands got big because they started with a simplification, not the other way round.
**Their tools:**
- **Screwdriver, not multi-tool.** Every tool on a multi-tool is worse than the real thing.
- **The Five Ones.** One problem, for one audience, one offer, one channel, for one year.
- **The Rule of One.** "Say one message a gazillion times, not a gazillion different messages once or twice."
- **Pick two.** You can only excel at two of cost, speed and quality. Choose by what your buyers value most.
- **The "Apple hotel" test.** If customers would happily follow you into an unrelated category, you stand for something. If not, you're known only for features, promotions and price.
**Examples:**
- The three chairs: red is Porsche, blue is Mercedes, green is Volvo.
- Steakhouse vs buffet.
- GEICO time and money. Progressive service. Allstate mayhem.
**Do:** Name the single idea and make everything ladder to it.
**Don't:** Lead with a menu of packages, services or segments.
**Plugs into:**
- Stage 4, Pass 3: each named strategy reduces to one idea.
- Strategy page §1, "the idea in a breath".
- Grader test 1. A possible new check: "Can the buyer say in one word what this brand is for?"

### 1.2 Escape the commodity trap
**Rule:** If everyone in your category makes the same claims, buyers treat you all as the same. They default to the "three dead ends": cheapest, fastest, or the name they already know.
**Their tests:**
- **Logo-swap test.** Remove the logos from the category's websites. "You wouldn't even know whose site you were on."
- **Sameness test.** Read the first thing on your top five competitors' sites and ads. If you all describe what you sell, you're a parity commodity.
- **Commodity checklist.** Is your team's case "we have a better product, better people, we work harder, we care more"? Those are table stakes, not differences.
- **Table stakes aren't differences.** "Customers love us", great customer service and quality are all expected. Every brand in the category has loyal fans.
**Why:** Sameness teaches buyers to price-shop. If you sound like the leader, you're advertising for the leader ("You mean like Michelin?").
**Examples:**
- The table of black chairs.
- Supplement brands that all say "science-based" against OLLY, which talks about the buyer's need.
- The weight-loss category.
- Agencies that all claim they're "strategic, data-driven, full-service".
**Do:** Start the market reading by admitting the product is a commodity (even Nike's), then ask how not to be seen as one.
**Don't:**
- Base a strategy on service, quality, care or customer love.
- Drift towards what the category leaders do ("beigeification").
**Plugs into:**
- Research Guide quick scan: run the sameness test on the alternatives' pitches.
- Grader test 3 (judgement words) and test 6 (only, not best).
- New grader check: **logo-swap test** on the strategy statement and the pick tagline.

### 1.3 Never compete on "better"
**Rule:** Never make "better" the strategy, whether better, faster, cheaper or newer (the "ER game").
**Why:**
- Buyers have no way to measure better beyond their own experience.
- Spec claims ("2x bigger", "15% fewer tangles") get beaten by a rival's 3x.
- Every advantage gets copied and becomes the baseline. "If you're trying to win with ER, you've already lost."
- People buy the product they like best or know best, not the best product. They buy on emotion and justify with logic.
**What to do instead:**
- You don't have to be objectively best to own an attribute. Volvo owns safety even though Mercedes is arguably safer, because Mercedes chose luxury.
- A good alternative angle is "look how much easier it is to get the same result with ours".
**Examples:**
- Volvo vs Mercedes.
- The Basilisk fang vs the Sword of Gryffindor.
- The pen that writes upside down.
**Do:** Give the buyer an easy-to-see reason to prefer you.
**Don't:**
- Lead with specs or "we're the best at X".
- Be "different for the sake of different". The difference must solve a problem for the buyer.
**Plugs into:** Grader test 6 (only, not best) and test 3. It strongly backs the book's "only is better than best".

### 1.4 Harmonize, don't compete: claim your own island
**Rule:** Map what each competitor owns, then claim an unoccupied territory and let the others keep theirs.
**Their tools:**
- **Never go head to head on ground a heavyweight already owns.** Don't launch a car as "the safest" against Volvo.
- **Competitor map.** Plot everyone on a 2x2 with two traits, such as calm vs bold and functional vs lifestyle. Find the empty quadrant.
- **Position where three things overlap:**
  - what you're good at and profitable doing
  - what competitors aren't doing
  - what a group of buyers wants
**Examples:**
- Insurance: GEICO, Progressive, Allstate and Farmers each own one "island of story".
- Water brands cluster on calm, functional and pure. Liquid Death sits alone in bold and lifestyle.
- A lawn business mapped against "Bob" (late), "Tommy" (oversells) and "Angelina".
**Do:**
- Draw the map before choosing.
- Study competitors in order to avoid them, not to imitate them.
**Don't:**
- Copy a successful competitor's message. A copycat with the same product and a "corporate meh" message loses (V.I.Poo vs Poo-Pourri).
**Plugs into:**
- Research Guide deep pass (the positioning map).
- Report Spec appendix, "Reading the market": draw the 2x2.
- Grader test 6, and test 4 (who it repels). Matches the book's "harmonize" and "declustering" (Toolkit §3).

### 1.5 Be the category's opposite
**Rule:** Find what every competitor is trying to own, then take the opposite position. When you're the same as the leader but less well known, "you're not an alternative, you're an afterthought" (RC Cola). Fight the bigger category, not your direct rival.
**Examples:**
- Water brands own "pure", so Liquid Death is the "unpure" rebel.
- 7UP's "The Un-Cola" against all colas.
- Whoop wins by not being a watch and not counting steps.
- Dollar Shave Club: "Don't pay for shave tech you don't need", less innovation, not more.
- Poppi reframed apple cider vinegar as "prebiotic soda".
**Do:**
- Express the anti-incumbent stance in every brand code.
- Commit to it. Watering a bold idea back towards the familiar walks you back to where you started.
**Don't:** Be a similar-but-cheaper version of the leader.
**Plugs into:**
- Stage 4, Pass 1: an extra angle generator, "what does every competitor claim, and what is its opposite?"
- Very close to the book's contrarian value method and the opposites game (Grader test 4).
- See conflict C6 on attacking competitors.

### 1.6 The three levels of marketing
**Rule:** Marketing works at three levels:
1. **Product.** Features anyone can copy. They put about 85% of businesses here. They compete on price.
2. **Problem and solution.** A feature that solves a specific problem for a specific group. About 10%. They can charge more.
3. **What you stand for.** The emotion behind the problem, which the brand stands for. About 5%. These are the category dominators.

Owning the emotion lets many products sit under one brand idea.
**Examples:**
- A pen that writes upside down becomes "writing solutions for difficult situations", then "never be frustrated again".
- Snickers went from "packed with peanuts" to "Snickers satisfies" to "You're not you when you're hungry".
**Do:** Check which level each draft strategy is written at.
**Don't:** Stop at a feature. That matches the grader's existing rule that a USP is not a strategy.
**Plugs into:**
- Stage 4, Pass 2: a useful ladder for moving a statement off a feature.
- Grader test 1.
- Watch conflict C2: level 3 can slide into fluff.
**Reach:** Their most-viewed teaching video (1.7M views) and repeated in 8 videos.

### 1.7 Narrow the audience, and say who it's not for
**Rule:** Decide at the start who you're for and who you're not for. Pick a very specific audience with a very specific need, never "anyone with a wallet".
**Why:**
- Growth comes from a subset that's "rabidly passionate" about what you offer.
- People who react badly to your stance aren't your audience. Don't soften the stance to win them.
- If someone sees your message and thinks "this isn't for me", good. "That means your message is finally clear."
**Examples:**
- Noggin, sunscreen for bald people.
- Nike's "Runners welcome, walkers tolerated", and Nike walking it back.
- A gym line: "No mirrors, no egos, just real results."
- Roofers: "pay a little more, fix it once" vs "a two-year fix for when money is tight".
- Starbucks people vs Dunkin' people.
**Do:**
- Name the buyer precisely, e.g. "growth-minded DTC brands under $3M ARR, not small businesses".
- Write a line that draws a line in the sand.
- Launch with a narrow, demanding core and widen later. YETI started with professional hunters and fishermen, which made it aspirational for weekend users. Then it added price points and distribution.
**Don't:** Stack qualifiers to appeal to everyone.
**Plugs into:**
- Strategy page §3 and §7 ("what it gives up and who it turns away").
- Grader test 4 (who it repels).
- Watch conflict C4 with test 7 (enough buyers).

### 1.8 Pick the pain, and ladder past the obvious answer
**Rule:** Your one job is to work out what you **make possible** or **make go away**.
- Ask "why would anybody care?" repeatedly until you pass the information and reach the real problem.
- "Sell the hole, not the drill" is only half right. Sell why they needed the hole.
- Prefer the pain you remove over the pleasure you add. People move away from pain more than towards pleasure.
- Make the cost of doing nothing concrete.
**Examples:**
- B2B promo videos that "even out the up-down cycle that drives you nuts all year", not "grow your business".
- Fleet GPS for "the vulnerability when you can't be in the truck".
- GEICO built on the pain the whole category ignored: people hate the insurance experience.
- An online course sold as "out of debt", not "more money".
**Do:** Choose a pain the category ignores. "The pain point you focus on is the linchpin to the entire thing."
**Don't:** Stop at the first, generic outcome. "That's novice level thinking."
**Plugs into:**
- Interview Guide: add the "why would anybody care?" ladder as a probe.
- Stage 4, Pass 1 (buying moments).
- Grader test 12 (would the buyer lean in).

## 2. Messaging: how it's said

### 2.1 Sell the outcome, not the product
**Rule:** Buyers buy *through* your product to what it gives them. Message the result, not the tool, and never just describe what you are. Their "what trap" is starting with what you do, how, and what it costs. Flip the order: open with the problem you solve, and explain the product only once they ask how.
**Their tools:**
- **"Make something possible or make something go away."**
- **The "so you can" line.** "Choose our product so you can ___", where the blank is the one benefit the buyer cares about most.
- **Translate specs into the buyer's unit.** Watt sold horsepower, not cylinder size. The iPod was "1,000 songs in your pocket", not 10GB.
- **The "other path" check.** They could have bought candy or wine instead of flowers. The product is one route to the outcome.
**Examples:**
- Hammer and nail vs the hung picture.
- The glasses hinge vs "glasses that don't break".
- Tinder: "75 million users, awards" vs a one-line promise of the result.
- "We offer high quality software" vs "cuts your reporting time in half so you can focus on growing".
- FedEx "absolutely, positively overnight" vs "99% overnight rate".
**Do:**
- Put the problem first, so the product becomes relevant.
- Keep features as the answer to "how?".
**Don't:** Lead with years in business, quality, a dedicated team or full service. Buyers "take it at face value... that's as far as they go."
**Plugs into:**
- Strategy statement (the "so [outcome]" clause).
- Core messages (§6).
- Grader test 11 (stranger test) and test 12.

### 2.2 Talk about them, not you
**Rule:** Never talk about yourself. Customers ask one question: what's in it for me? Make the buyer the hero, already mid-story with a problem. Show you understand their situation better than anyone before you mention the product. "Talking about yourself in marketing is about as effective as talking about yourself at a cocktail party."
**Examples:**
- **The tow truck story**, their signature (541k views). Two drivers, same company. One talks about history, awards and kit. The other says: "Sit tight. Armato is on the road in 15 minutes or less. We know you've got places to be."
- A doctor: "20 years' experience" vs "if you wake up with shoulder pain...".
- Monogram's "Elevate everything" as self-congratulation.
**Founder stories:** "Your brand story is not your founder's story. Nobody cares about your founder's story."
**Their test:** the "emphatic hell yes". The buyer feels "they get me, where have you been all my life".
**Do:**
- Strip out "me, us, we".
- Describe the buyer's moment in the words they already use.
**Don't:**
- Use awards, heritage or origin stories as messaging.
- Run award announcements as ads. "This ad is for the people in the hospital."
**Plugs into:**
- Taglines trap 2, already in [Taglines](taglines.md).
- Core messages.
- Strategy page §3 (the buyer's moment).
- Grader test 12.
- The founder story belongs in §11 "Why it matters", never in messages.

### 2.3 Clear beats clever
**Rule:** If buyers have to think, you've lost them.
- **Simple feels safe.** The clearest brands beat the cleverest. "The brain associates a lack of clarity with risk."
- **The 10-second rule.** If you can't hook someone on why you're right for them in 10 seconds, you've lost them.
- **People scan, they don't read.** They scan in an F-shaped pattern, so make it scannable.
- **The curse of knowledge.** "You can't read your own label from inside the jar." Drop insider words like "integrated end-to-end solutions" and "proprietary framework".
**Their tests:**
- Would a five-year-old, or your mum, get it?
- Reread it "blind" as someone outside the industry.
- Cut every sentence in half.
- "If your audience can't remember it, they can't repeat it."
**Why:** Good marketing "makes the buyer feel smarter for understanding you", not you sound smart.
**Do:** "We help people with this exact problem do this exact solution so they get this exact benefit."
**Don't:**
- Use vague claims that invite "so what?" ("Socks loved by pros": which pros?).
- Borrow another brand's famous line ("There is no substitute" is Porsche's).
**Plugs into:** Grader test 11 (stranger test) and the Report Spec quality bar ("no insider words"). It confirms rules the skill already has, with their tests as extra prompts for the grader.

### 2.4 The elevator pitch: three questions
**Rule:** An elevator pitch answers exactly three questions, a sentence or two each, in under 20 seconds (some videos say 10):
1. **What are you?** A plain, context-setting description, e.g. "a dentist for people over 80".
2. **What problem do you solve?** Why you exist, not what you do.
3. **How are you different?** A problem your competitors ignore because they're busy describing themselves, and what you do about it. Not a self-description, and not price.
**Why:** It's "an efficiency tool, not a sales tool". It lets listeners opt in or out fast. It works when they start describing their own pain back to you. "If you've got more than a sentence or two for any one of these, it's too long."
**Their variants:**
- Problem, then promise, then solution (each quality tied to a pain), then results.
- "Who are we really for? What do we believe? What do we do differently? Why does that difference matter?"
- A value proposition must say the problem, why you're different in a way the buyer cares about, and be sayable simply.
**Do:** Push credentials (years, team size) to the bottom, used only when asked.
**Don't:**
- Use the oversimplified label ("I'm an attorney").
- Wander into a diatribe.

### 2.5 One promise, exactly three proofs
**Rule:** Put every other message underneath one big promise. Once you have the promise, "you are only allowed to say that you do it in three ways". Credentials and supporting facts go at the bottom. Sales and marketing must use the same promise-and-proof structure.
**Proof must be specific:**
- A customer who looks like the one you want.
- The problem they had, what changed, and the result, in words the next buyer recognises.
- Not "Brand Boss was amazing".
- Avoid proof statistics that can be picked apart (Colgate's "4 out of 5 dentists").
- Best of all, let the customer be the proof (Shot on iPhone).
**Examples:**
- Volvo: safety, proven by the three-point seat belt, crumple zones and crash-test work.
- A contractor: "If we don't show up on time, you don't pay a dime", plus three ways.
- Nike stands for "progress" and cascades it to runners, swimmers and pros.
**Do:**
- One promise.
- Three proofs.
- The same words in sales and marketing.
**Don't:** Message each audience separately at product level.

### 2.6 Never get bored of your message
**Rule:** Pick one idea and reinforce it from every angle. Vary the stories, examples and proof, never the message.
**Why:**
- "The worst possible mistake you can make at this level is getting bored of your own message and deciding to change it." Every message change resets recognition to zero.
- Consistency builds expectation, expectation builds reputation, reputation builds the brand.
- You'll be sick of it long before buyers notice it.
- Ads rarely wear out. They cite a study: 14 out of 50,000.
**Consistency has three parts:**
- identity: the same logo and colours everywhere
- message: the one thing you stand for
- experience
**Examples:**
- Nike "Just Do It".
- Apple, from "1984" through "Think Different" to Mac vs PC.
- Coke selling "joy in a bottle" for decades.
**Do:** Run a message at least three months before judging it.
**Don't:**
- A/B-test new headlines weekly.
- Drop price when something stalls.
**Plugs into:**
- Main note principle 6 ("hold the strategy for ever").
- Strategy page §8–9: first moves should include "say it the same way everywhere".
- Strongly agrees with the book (Toolkit §9).

### 2.7 Interesting or ignored

## 3. Story and creative

### 3.1 Every brand story needs an enemy and a hero
**Rule:** "You need an enemy and you need a hero. That dynamic tension is what allows us to care." The buyer is the hero, the problem or status quo is the enemy, and your brand helps them win.
**Their tests:**
- If a campaign has nothing leadership could object to and makes no bold claim for or against anything, "there's no enemy, so there can be no hero".
- Great advertising "aligns people with a belief" the audience already holds. "Never try to convince anybody of anything."
**Examples:**
- TOMS (one for one).
- Dollar Shave Club vs overpriced shave tech.
- Wendy's "Where's the beef?".
- Apple "1984" vs IBM as Big Brother.
- Nike "Winning isn't for everybody".
**Building the story:**
- Base it on the biggest, most consistent pain across the audience, not on features or origin.
- **Four story tests:**
  - relevant
  - simple enough a five-year-old could retell it
  - memorable
  - easy to share
- Pick the story first, before colours, logo or fonts.
**Do:**
- Name the enemy in each strategy, even if it's a habit or the status quo.
- Sometimes the enemy is a belief about the category. For a flavoured tequila it was "flavoured tequila is bad", so "our berry is the berriest" missed the problem.
**Don't:** Tell the story of a blender's quick-release lid.
**Plugs into:**
- Strategy page §4, the big idea: "how things are today, why that falls short" is the enemy.
- Grader test 9 (challenger trap) and the book's true-competitor idea. The enemy is often the true competitor (Toolkit §5).

### 3.2 Find the big idea, then play "if this, then that"
**Rule:** Winning brands use the same elements as losing ones. The difference is a big idea, not a big budget.

  - unmistakably on brand
  - flexible across formats
  - cheap to make

- KitKat "Have a break".

## 4. Brand

### 4.1 A brand is an expectation, not a logo
**Rule:** "A brand is simply the expectation someone has of what they're going to get from buying from or working with your business." Logo, fonts and colours only represent it.
- Narrative sets the expectation.
- A good experience meeting it creates reputation.
- Reputation spreads by word of mouth.

**Definitions they use:**
- Branding is knowing *what* you're saying and why.
- Marketing is knowing *where* and *how* to say it.
- Advertising is buying space and filling it.

**Their tools:**
- **Write the promise first.** Write the promise before building anything. Quest wrote "a protein bar that tastes like candy but is good for you". Then list everything that must be true to deliver it.
- **Write the review.** Write the review you want your best customer to leave, and work backwards.
- **Decide the word of mouth.** Decide what you want people to say about you "when you're not in the room", and design the experience to make them say it.
- **One signature extra.** Deliver the promise, then add one small signature extra people come to expect.
**Don't:** Build the promise from table stakes (trust, quality, integrity, innovation).
**Plugs into:**
- Strategy page §8 and §12. "Write the review first" is a cheap test of a strategy.
- The book's strategic hierarchy already separates strategy, delivery and branding. See conflict C7.

### 4.4 Price is a positioning signal
**Rule:** Don't drop prices or lead with discounts. It positions you as a price-first commodity, trains buyers to wait for the sale, and brings in disloyal deal-chasers. "If you want a discount on a Porsche, it's called used."
**What to do instead:**
- Add value: a bonus, a bundle or exclusive access.
- If you must discount, attach conditions, because harder-to-get discounts feel bigger.
**Premium means restraint.** "Premium brands don't discount at all."
**Price is a filter.** Price on value, and don't chase people who undervalue you. If the only complaint is price, "you're perfect, don't change a thing".
**Most price objections are value objections.** A weak message makes $500 feel expensive. A strong one makes $5,000 feel cheap.
**Signals that set price:**
- Presentation: wine in a plastic cup ($8), a stemmed glass ($17) or poured by a waiter ($21).
- Packaging and colour: saturated primaries read mass market, muted tones read premium.
- Scarcity: Ferrari caps output, Hermès stops popular items.
- One iconic protected price: Costco's $1.50 hot dog.

### 4.5 Look and feel rules
**Rules:**
- **Logos.** A logo doesn't need to explain what you do. It should be simple and memorable and capture a feeling. "Your logo doesn't need to tell your story." As expectations take hold, simplify the mark.
- **Owned colour.** Own a colour and use the squint test: is it recognisable from a distance? Crumbl pink.
- **The 60/30/10 rule.** 60% primary colour, 30% secondary, 10% accent.
- **Choosing colour.** Pick it for the emotion and price point you want, not at random.
- **Photography and stock images.** Don't cut corners on photography. Avoid stock lifestyle shots nobody can see themselves in.
- **Packaging's three jobs.** Who it's for, how they should feel using it, and where it lives (on the counter as decor or under the sink).
- **Mascots.** A mascot or character makes a brand human and memorable.
- **Different with purpose.** Break the category's visual pattern, but make the difference easier to understand, use or value, "or it's just decoration" (the Graza squeeze bottle).

## 5. Taglines, naming and architecture

### 5.1 Taglines: additions to the Taglines note
The four types and two traps are already in [Taglines](taglines.md). Their other tagline videos add:
- **Length.** "Make sure that the tagline is no more than five [words]. If you're able to get it down to three, you get bonus points."
- **Internal vs external.** A purpose statement (no more than 10 words) rallies the team. The tagline rallies customers. Translate one into the other; don't broadcast the internal version.
  - Disney: "recapture the magic of childhood" becomes "The happiest place on earth".
  - Las Vegas: "sin safely" becomes "What happens here stays here".
- **The tagline is "the summation of the promise against the problem".** Write it after the problem and promise, as their short form.
- **Description is not a tagline.** "If your tagline is a description of you, rethink it."
- **Generic vs differentiated.** Replace accurate-but-generic lines with differentiated ones that provoke:
  - "We help companies grow" becomes "We help underdog founders punch above their weight class".
  - "Premium hydration" becomes "Murder your thirst".
- **Directive is their favourite structure.** Build it on a fundamental human truth ("Just do it" came from Gary Gilmore's "Let's do it"). In one older video they say only two forms work, directive or promise. The four-type masterclass supersedes that.
- **A name-carrying line can work.** "Protect your Noggin" repeats the name and says what it does.

- [Taglines](taglines.md) and Grader test 13.
- Candidate additions:
  - a **five-word maximum**, with three preferred
  - a **purpose statement** line kept internal, for §11 "Why it matters"
  - the "promise against the problem" check

### 5.2 Naming
**Rules:**
- **The knock test.** Two or three syllables with an up-down rhythm are easiest to remember and fun to say. Four is the most. One is memorable but not playful.
- **Pick the short form yourself.** Long names get shortened anyway (Federal Express became FedEx, Coca-Cola became Coke).
- **Avoid the generic category name.** Use a playful, ownable name instead of "<Owner>'s <Category> Service". Yardrific or Lawn For You beats Bob's Lawn Service, and it wins search.
- **Drop the category word as you grow.** Crumbl Cookies became Crumbl, Starbucks Coffee became Starbucks.
- **Don't drop the part that carries the meaning.** HBO Max to Max threw away "HBO = premium storytelling".
- **A name needs to be appropriate, not meaningful.** The expectations you build give it meaning ("Wendy's" means burgers). Avoid initialisms.
- **Don't force the name into a verb** when it doesn't naturally work.
- **Mine the name for brand codes.** Three Brothers Coffee can become three characters with storylines.
- **Make the name, logo and tagline reinforce one strategic choice** (Noggin).
**Plugs into:**
- Stage 4, Pass 3, if a strategy implies a new product or sub-brand name.
- Strategy page §10.

### 5.3 Brand architecture
**Rule:** With more than one product, choose deliberately:
- **Branded house.** One master brand (Apple, Nike). Equity compounds, but everything rises and falls together.
- **House of brands.** The parent stays behind the curtain (P&G, Unilever). Each brand owns its own position, and risk is isolated.
- **Hybrid or endorsed.** Sub-brands with their own audience and price, plus master-brand credibility ("Courtyard by Marriott").
**Then:**
- Define each brand's role.
- Set naming conventions (descriptive, endorsed or standalone) and apply them consistently.
- Write a messaging hierarchy of what the master brand and each sub-brand stand for.
"If you can't explain the difference clearly, the market isn't going to understand it either." Clarity first, structure second.
**Two positioning shapes to choose between up front:**
- **Inline.** Known for one thing, done better than anyone (Porsche performance, "There is no substitute").
- **Umbrella.** One idea delivered across many products and audiences (Nike progress, "Just do it").
**Don't stretch a brand into categories it wasn't built for.** Launch a separate brand instead (Honda to Acura). Keep additions rare and limited (the McRib), not permanent menu creep.
**Plugs into:**
- Stage 1 homework for multi-product businesses (a family of products).
- The phase-2 document.
- Not needed for single-product sessions.

## 6. Process

### 6.1 Strategy before tactics; you can't A/B test your way to a position
**Rule:** Build in order:
1. research
2. strategy (a position unique in the category)
3. the story that brings it to life
4. only then tactics, logo, colours, fonts, website and tagline

Those are "expressions of the brand, not its engine".
**A/B testing:** "You can't A/B test your way out of a positioning problem." Eddie Murphy decided to be the funniest man alive. Testing which joke landed best on Tuesday was execution.
**Other rules:**
- One strategic decision at the start "eliminates randomness" from the hundred after it (Noggin).
- Match the brand job to the stage:
  - to $1M: prove it, with one audience, one message and one offer
  - to $10M: build preference
  - to $100M: own an idea in the mind
- Strategy works as guardrails. Write a do's and don'ts list from it and decide against it, not in the moment. Run new ideas through four questions:
  - does it fit the vision?
  - does it use our real strengths?
  - is there real demand?
  - is it financially viable?
**Plugs into:**
- Main note principles 3–4.
- Strategy page §8 first moves.
- A "brand guardrails" list (do's and don'ts) could be a useful addition to the phase-2 document.
- Agrees with the book.

### 6.2 Research the real reason they buy
**Rule:** Don't take a brand to market on assumptions, sales-team anecdotes or your own taste. "You are not your target audience."
**How to research:**
- Don't ask "what would make our product better?" Ask why, when, where and under what circumstances they buy, and what they love and hate about buying.
- Ask what they hate about the current way, not what they want ("faster horses").
- Look past the stated reason to the real driver. Jiffy Lube's mums said the car was their second-biggest investment, but what they wanted was the car vacuumed and the windows cleaned.
- Ask what assumptions the whole category makes that make everyone sound the same.
- Find out which alternatives *outside* your category buyers weigh before they buy. This is the book's "true competitor".
- Judge messaging by whether the audience likes it, not the CEO.
**Plugs into:**
- Research Guide §4 (buyer voice).
- Interview Guide probes.
- Grader test 7 (wanted, in buyers' own words).
- See conflict C3.

## 7. Conflicts with the book

Default: **the book wins on what the strategy is; Brand Boss wins on how it's said.** Each **Decided** line is now a rule in the Grader.

**C1. Start with why.**
- *Brand Boss:* calls *Start With Why* "the greatest marketing book that I think I ever read". They have a purpose statement written before the tagline or website ("why do we exist beyond making money?"), and says a why bigger than the product lets the brand stretch.
  - Sources: [7429458295842245919](https://www.tiktok.com/@brandbosshq/video/7429458295842245919), [7503607330735476010](https://www.tiktok.com/@brandbosshq/video/7503607330735476010).
- *The book:* a why is a motivation tool, not strategy. Sinek's Apple why is "strategically empty" (Toolkit §9).
- *Brand Boss contradicts themselves:* in a later video, captioned "Don't start with Why. Start with What", they say to start with what you want people to say about you when you're not in the room ([7418918257219882283](https://www.tiktok.com/@brandbosshq/video/7418918257219882283)).
- **Decided:** the book wins. Their purpose statement goes in "Why it matters", kept apart from the strategy, and is never used as a message.

**C2. "Stand for an emotion" and level 3 of marketing.**
- *Brand Boss:* the less a brand is about the product, the faster it grows. Stand for an emotional state ("never frustrated", "joy in a bottle", "freedom"). The top 5% "stand for something".
- *The book:* emotional or lofty statements are the classic "pretty strategy" that demands nothing (Toolkit §7, error 1). The **toilet paper rule** says low-interest categories need one blunt promise, not a rich brand world. The **challenger trap** says purpose and activism are yesterday's challengers.
- **Decided:** the book wins on the strategy statement, which must still pass the change and subjectivity tests. Their level 3 is welcome in the big idea, taglines and voice.

**C3. Research.**
- *Brand Boss:* never go to market on assumptions. Do qualitative and quantitative research, and build the strategy from consumer insight.
- *The book:* "You can never research your way to a strategy." The evidence is mostly observation, and the strategy is found in a chat.
- *Partly reconcilable:* their research questions (why, when, what they hate, the real driver) are good buyer-voice probes for test 7. **Decided:** their side on doing it. The skill gathers buyer voice every time and uses their questions as probes. Research never stands in for choosing the strategy.

**C4. A narrow audience vs "enough buyers want it".**
- *Brand Boss:* narrow to a "rabidly passionate" subset, and accept that most people aren't for you.
- *The book and Grader test 7:* the value must be wanted by a lot of people and be big enough to shape the business.
- *Brand Boss also says* to pick the largest value persona from research. **Decided:** keep their narrow niche, but it must still pass test 7 (enough buyers). Their "who it's not for" is the test 4 repel line.

**C5. "Pick what you enjoy and profit from most".**
- *Brand Boss:* choose the one thing from what you do best, enjoy and make the most money from. Choose the outcome "by where you make the most profit and get the most joy, not by scanning problem statements and data".
- *The book:* broadly agrees (the Dolly Principle, "intensify where the business leans"). But the book adds the market side: uniqueness and true competitors.
- **Decided:** the book's Venn diagram of market and business governs.

**C6. Attacking competitors.**
- *Brand Boss:* sometimes challenge the category by calling out the leader's weakness, Wendy's "Where's the beef?" style. Elsewhere they say to harmonize.
- *The book:* the non-competition mindset. Complement competitors and respect what they're good at (Toolkit §3, §5).
- **Decided:** the book wins. Name the buyer's enemy (a status quo, habit or belief), never a competitor. A strategy defined by beating a rival fails test 14.

**C7. What "brand" means.**
- *Brand Boss:* a brand is the expectation of an experience or outcome, and "brand strategy" covers the promise, story and position together.
- *The book:* separates strategy (unique value), delivery and branding (communication).
- *Mostly vocabulary.* **Decided:** keep the book's three layers; their "brand promise" is the strategy summary or tagline.

**C8. Category colour codes.**
- *Brand Boss:* match the colour and font cues buyers have been trained to expect for the category ([7380372908306140446](https://www.tiktok.com/@brandbosshq/video/7380372908306140446)).
- *The book:* copy the brand codes of businesses with similar value in *other* categories, to be "familiar but different". Copy your own category's codes only when normalising something weird (Toolkit §9).
- *They also says* to break the category's visual pattern with purpose. **Decided:** both. Keep the cues buyers expect in the category, and borrow tone and voice from brands in other categories.

**C9. Brand Boss contradicts themselves (not book conflicts, but flagged):**
- **The squint test.** "Use the squint test" ([7371074348716920110](https://www.tiktok.com/@brandbosshq/video/7371074348716920110)) vs "The squint test is nonsense" for redesigns ([7468306796667702574](https://www.tiktok.com/@brandbosshq/video/7468306796667702574)).
- **Redesigns.** Tropicana-style "leave recognisable packaging alone" vs "a redesign should be bold and distinctive even if it breaks conventions".
- **Tagline forms.** Two forms (directive or promise) in 2023 vs four types in 2026. The skill already uses four.
- **Motivators.** Three different lists of what people are "motivated by" (see 4.2).

**No conflict, and worth noting:** their rules on consistency ("never get bored of your message") and on not abandoning the core match the book's "hold the strategy for ever, keep changing the execution" (Toolkit §9). Their "one promise, three proofs" fits the skill's "three core messages, each with proof".
