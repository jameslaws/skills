# Research Guide

Research has one job in this method: to show where the market is crowded, where it's empty, and what the business could own. It is not there to produce a market-size deck. The book is blunt that "you can never research your way to a strategy" (p.82). Research supplies the raw material, and the conversation and judgement do the rest.

Run research through background helpers (subagents) so the interview keeps moving. **Run no more than 2–3 helpers at once.** Each helper gets one of the briefs below, plus the name of the business, what it does in a sentence, and the session's research folder path.

## Sourcing rules (for every helper)

- **Every outside claim needs a link and the date it was checked.** Competitor positioning should quote the page itself: the homepage headline, the tagline, pricing tiers.
- **Mark facts and inferences separately.** "WPForms homepage H1: 'Drag & Drop WordPress Form Builder'" is a fact. "WPForms owns beginner-friendly" is an inference. Label it.
- **Use primary sources first:** the competitor's own site, pricing page, changelog, review listings (wordpress.org, G2, Capterra, the App Store), and public numbers such as install counts. Use roundup and listicle sites only to find names, never as evidence.
- **Keep it short.** Return tables and bullets, not essays. The report will cite these.
- **Budget the searches.** A run can hit a cap on web searches. Spend no more than about two thirds on the quick scan and the deep pass. Keep the rest for checking each Move's evidence and for a replacement Move. When searches run out, fetch known pages directly (pricing pages, forum threads, government statistics) and list what couldn't be checked. A spent budget never justifies dropping a strategy. (The 2026-10-30 run used up its searches on the last evidence pass and shipped one Move short.)
- **Don't let research replace the founder.** If research seems to contradict their account, report the conflict. Don't resolve it.

## 1. Quick scan: start it during homework, before the first question

> **Brief:** "Map the category <Thing> competes in. Return:
> (a) the category name buyers would use;
> (b) the 5–8 main alternatives buyers actually consider, including free and non-brand options. For each give the name, URL, homepage headline or tagline quoted, pricing model, and who it seems built for;
> (c) the 3–6 values nearly every competitor leads with (the category norms), with quotes as evidence;
> (d) the sameness check: quote the first line of each alternative's homepage side by side, and say whether you could swap the logos and not notice;
> (e) anything surprising you noticed.
> Cite everything."

Use the output to sharpen your interview questions. Don't present it to the founder as a finding yet.

## 2. Targeted lookups, during the interview

Send one when a name or an idea comes up. Keep each brief to one question:
- **A named competitor or alternative:** how it positions itself (quoted), pricing, who it's for, what reviewers praise and complain about.
- **A possible nearby category** (a context shift idea): what buyers there value, the main brands and their weaknesses, and whether <Thing> would have a natural edge there.
- **A reference brand** the founder mentions: what it actually offers and how it shows that.
- **A claim to check** ("nobody else does X"): does anyone? Show the evidence.

## 3. Deep pass, after the interview and before building

Split this across 2–3 helpers. Always include the buyer-voice brief (§4) and the gap hunt (§5). Together they produce:

1. **The positioning map.**
   - For each competitor, its main claimed value, from its own homepage and pricing, quoted.
   - Group them into clusters (brands sitting on top of each other).
   - Note where the open space is.
   - Name the two dimensions that best separate the market (for example price vs depth, beginner vs developer, all-in-one vs specialist). The report draws the map on these two dimensions.
2. **Category norms.** The values everyone competes on, with evidence. These are the "locked-in assumptions" that unexpected or contrarian value could break.
3. **True competitors by reason for buying.** For each main reason people buy, what else satisfies that need, including non-brand options, and how <Thing> compares with each.
4. **The gap hunt (§5).** Always its own helper. It finds the open ground the Moves are built from.
5. **Status quo and yesterday's challengers.** Who sets today's norms? Which once-insurgent brands are now establishment? What do they all agree on? (For the challenger trap.)
6. **Brands in other categories offering similar value.** For each strategy direction the founder leaned towards, two or three brands in *other* categories that offer similar value, plus the visual and verbal shorthand they've built. Customers already recognise that shorthand, so it can be borrowed (cross-category copying).
7. **How much buyers care.** Evidence of how much time and attention they put into this purchase: review depth, community size, how often people switch, price sensitivity.
8. **Facts that matter.** Market size, growth or pricing benchmarks, *only* if they change a decision. Skip them otherwise.
9. **The price map.** What the buyer pays today, from each alternative's own pricing page (dated): for each main alternative, and for the pile of tools a strategy would replace, at two or three realistic company sizes. Note how each one charges (per person, flat, bands, usage) and any price jumps buyers complain about. Each strategy's price stance and sizing start from this one table, so the numbers agree.

Save the deep-pass output as `<Thing> Strategy Research YYYY-MM-DD.md` beside the strategy note. The report's market section and sources list are built from it.

## 4. Buyer voice: what actually makes people buy or switch

Run this during the deep pass, every time. Without it the options get built from the founder's view alone. An early run (2026-10-23) produced lines no buyer would care about because nothing in it captured why buyers actually move.

> **Brief:** "Find the buyers' own words, not vendor claims. Look at forums (Reddit, Hacker News, niche communities), G2, Capterra and App Store reviews, and 'why we switched' posts, filtered to buyers like <Thing>'s. Return:
> (a) the 6–10 most common reasons they switch or buy, each with 2–3 verbatim quotes and links;
> (b) the words they use for the outcome they want, plus the phrases that recur;
> (c) what they fear about switching;
> (d) which pains are strongest, and which are just complaining;
> (e) what they hate about the way they do it today, and what they weighed besides products like this (spreadsheets, hiring, doing nothing).
> Verbatim quotes only, each with a link. Label inferences."

Use it to:
- write the "who [problem]" half of every strategy statement in the buyer's own words;
- name each option's buying moment;
- test whether taglines use words buyers actually use.

## 5. The gap hunt: where the product could go

Run this during the deep pass, every time, as its own helper. The buyer-voice brief looks at the buyers the product already has in mind. This one looks around them. A run without it (2026-10-29) searched only where the product already was and shipped one strategy.

> **Brief:** "<Thing> is <one sentence>. Its real strengths are <2–4 from the interview and homework: what it does well, who it knows, what it's built on>. Find the underserved spaces near it, **whether or not <Thing> serves them today**. Look for:
> (a) **buyers the category ignores or prices out**: too small, too big, an unusual industry or shape, a region, a stage of business;
> (b) **jobs people do with workarounds**: spreadsheets, a hire, duct-taped tools, doing without. Look for 'we just use a spreadsheet for…' and 'I wish X also…' in forums and reviews;
> (c) **incumbents retreating**: price rises, dropped plans, a pivot upmarket, a product getting worse, a takeover, with the complaints that followed;
> (d) **shifts creating new needs**: technology (AI, agents), regulation, how people work, new kinds of company;
> (e) **nearby categories where <Thing>'s strengths are rare**: where it would be the odd one out with an advantage.
> For each gap (aim for 8–12), return: the gap in a phrase; who is underserved; the evidence, with verbatim quotes and links; who is there now and why they leave it open; which of <Thing>'s strengths would carry over; what <Thing> would need to build or change; a rough size (how many buyers, what they pay today), with a source for every count and every ratio labelled sourced or guessed. Label inferences. Don't filter for what <Thing> does today; that's the point."

Use it to:
- score every gap (main note, Stage 4, Pass 3: acted pain, size, carry-over, distance, defensibility); the two best that pass the Move rule become the Moves, and each Move rests on one gap;
- fill the More directions tab with the strong gaps that didn't become full strategies;
- draw the positioning map's open ground on the Overview.
