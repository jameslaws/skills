# Patterns: what a real strategy looks like, taken apart

Every case below has the same five parts a core needs. Read them before writing a single core, and copy the *moves*, not the companies. Each one names a buyer you could point at in the street, and none of them is a feature list.

## The cases

**Starbucks (pp.19–20, 91)**
- Everyone assumed: a coffee shop sells coffee, fast.
- The reframe: the true competitor is the office and the home. Sell a third place between them.
- The buyer: people who need somewhere to be between work and home.
- What it gave up: table turnover. People are invited to stay for hours.
- What it changed: armchairs, power sockets, Wi-Fi, music, store design, staff behaviour.

**Southwest (pp.59, 88–89, 106–107)**
- Everyone assumed: airlines win by fighting for business travellers.
- The reframe: compete with the bus and the car.
- The buyer: people who'd otherwise drive or take the bus between nearby cities.
- What it gave up: business travellers, business class, lounges and hub airports.
- What it changed: one aircraft type, point-to-point routes, fast turnarounds, rock-bottom fares, jokey cabin crew.

**IKEA (pp.32, 128–130)**
- Everyone assumed: good design is for people with money.
- The reframe: designer interiors accessible to everyone regardless of wealth or background.
- The buyer: young households furnishing small homes on small budgets.
- What it gave up: delivery, assembly and city-centre shops.
- What it changed: flat-pack, out-of-town stores, designs for small spaces and kids, the catalogue.

**Tesla (pp.142, 173–181)**
- Everyone assumed: electric cars mean frugality and compromise.
- The reframe: electric cars as symbols of luxury and performance.
- The buyer: wealthy drivers who want the fastest, newest thing.
- What it gave up: economy buyers, at first.
- What it changed: the sports car first, then the luxury saloon, Apple-style showrooms, no dealers.

**Nintendo Switch (pp.105–106)**
- Everyone assumed: consoles compete on graphics power.
- The reframe: one console you can play on the TV and take on the train.
- The buyer: players who game in short bursts, wherever they are.
- What it gave up: power. It deliberately underperformed rivals.
- What it changed: the hybrid hardware, detachable controllers, local multiplayer, the games it commissioned.

**The cereal bar (pp.85–87)**
- Everyone assumed: a cereal bar competes as a health bar, where it was one of the least healthy.
- The reframe (context shift): sell it as confectionery, "the unhealthy person's health food".
- The buyer: people reaching for a chocolate bar who'd like to feel a bit better about it.
- What it gave up: credibility on the health shelf.
- What it changed: placement in corner shops and vending machines. Its sugar became a strength. The product didn't change.

**Method and Xero (pp.95–98)**
- Everyone assumed: cleaning products and accounting software only need to work.
- The reframe (unexpected value): make them beautiful.
- The buyer: people who'd leave a beautiful bottle on the counter; small-business owners who hate how their books look and feel.
- What it gave up: the cheap, purely functional end of the shelf.
- What it changed: packaging and interface design came first, and Method's look reinforced its eco claims.

**Airbnb (pp.101–103)**
- Everyone assumed: Airbnb was about cheap rooms.
- The reframe (found inside the business): guests were really buying belonging, not being a tourist.
- The buyer: travellers who want to live like a local.
- What it gave up: competing with hotels on price and consistency.
- What it changed: "Belong Anywhere", hosts as the brand, experiences.

**Savannah Bananas (pp.113–114)**
- Everyone assumed: a baseball team competes on baseball.
- The reframe (cultivate a weakness): spend less on the baseball and more on the show.
- The buyer: families who find baseball boring.
- What it gave up: serious baseball fans.
- What it changed: rules, stunts, dancing players, fan experience, ticket demand.

**Dog food for pensioners (pp.144–146)**
- Everyone assumed: "be number one for pensioners" was a strategy.
- The reframe (how cascade): pensioners disproportionately own lap dogs, so make the food for lap dogs.
- The buyer: older owners of small dogs.
- What it gave up: big-dog owners.
- What it changed: portion sizes, kibble size, pack sizes, where it's sold.

**Curves (p.208)**
- Everyone assumed: a gym needs mirrors, locker rooms and serious kit.
- The reframe: a 30-minute circuit for women who feel out of place in a gym.
- The buyer: women who've never felt comfortable in a gym.
- What it gave up: men, serious lifters and locker rooms.
- What it changed: the whole floor plan, the circuit and the staff.

## What all of them share

- **A buyer you can point at.** Commuters, wealthy early adopters, families bored by baseball. Never "small businesses", "teams" or "owners of 5–30 person companies". Pointable doesn't mean tiny: Southwest's buyer was most of the people driving between Texas cities. Name the group concretely, then check it's big enough for the goal.
- **A reframe of what's being bought,** often by naming a surprising true competitor (the office, the bus, the chocolate bar).
- **A sacrifice a sensible rival wouldn't make,** and it hurts.
- **It changes the whole business,** not one screen.

## The bland patterns (what to throw out)

These come straight from failed runs:
- **The generic owner.** "Owners of small businesses", "leaders who care about their people". You can't list them, you can't find them, and every rival claims them. Name an industry, a profession, a community or a kind of organisation instead, with real examples.
- **Features shown in more places.** "Receipts attached to every invoice, on every screen." This is delivery, not strategy.
- **The founder's own pitch, reworded.** "All in one place", "simple", "built for teams like ours". If the founder said it in the interview, it's the floor.
- **A clever premise with nothing behind it.** "Every meeting capped at 15 minutes" is a mechanism dressed as a sacrifice. The sacrifice has to cost the target buyer something they want.
- **A substitute for a person, priced as software.** "Instead of hiring a bookkeeper." Buyers rarely pay software prices for what they now pay people to do unless the software plainly does that person's job.
