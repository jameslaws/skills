# Interview Guide

The book's best tool is the informal chat (Toolkit §6). Chats work because they are opinion-based, candid, a little pressured (two people, nowhere to hide) and free to wander. Run the interview the same way: as a conversation you steer, not a form the founder fills in.

## How to run it

- **One question per message.** Keep it to one or two sentences. If a question needs context, give one line of what you found, then ask. ("Your homepage leads with 'one place for everything'. Is that the thing people actually buy?")
- **Skip what the homework already answered.** Confirm it in passing if it matters ("I read that X; still true?"), then move on.
- **Follow the live thread.** The stages below are a map, not a script. If the founder says something surprising, chase it. Surprise is where strategy comes from.
- **Push past vague answers.** If an answer contains "better", "easier", "quality", "great", "trusted", "community", or "people love it", ask one of these:
  - "How, specifically?"
  - "Better than what, at what?"
  - "Would a customer say that without being prompted?"
  - "What would you see from the outside that proves it?"
- **Ask about the real past, never hypotheticals.** Don't ask the founder to imagine or predict what some other buyer would do ("picture a café owner…"). Ask what actually happened: their own past switches, real customer conversations, what they're watched their team do. Use research for what strangers do.
- **Ask for opinions.** "What's your gut on…", "What do you secretly think…". Strategy lives in opinions about how facts fit together.
- **Write it down as you go.** Record each answer word for word in the strategy note's session record, with your distilled reading beneath it. Don't paraphrase their words.
- **Check in now and then.** Every 4–5 questions, give one line on where you've got to ("So far: it's really X for Y, and you're willing to lose Z"), so the founder can correct course.

## The path

Aim for 8–15 questions in total. Draw from each stage what the homework didn't already cover.

### 1. What it really is (value, not product)
- "In a sentence: what is it, and who pays for it?"
- "Forget features. What does a customer actually *get* from it? What changes for them?"
- "When a customer is happiest with it, what just happened?"

### 2. The actual path vs the imagined path (the Dolly Principle)
- "When you started, what did you think you were building? What has it actually turned into?"
- "What's worked better than you expected: a feature, a customer type, a channel, a use you didn't plan for?"
- "What flopped that you were sure would work?"
- "What do customers praise without being asked? What do they complain about?"
- "Is there anything about what this business has become that you secretly wish it wasn't?" (This checks for the self-loathing business. If the answer is yes, dig in.)

### 2b. Buying moments (real ones)
- "Tell me about the last time you (or a customer) actually switched to or from something like this. What was happening that week?"
- "What was the line, the demo or the moment that made you say yes?" If they doesn't remember, ask what pain they already had.
- "What do you say when you describe it to a friend who runs a similar business?" Their plain words are tagline material.
- **Ladder past the first answer.** When they name a pain or an outcome, ask "and why did that matter to them?" until you reach the real problem, usually two or three rounds. "Saves time" is never the bottom. (Brand Boss: "That's novice level thinking.")
- "What did they hate about the way they were doing it before?" Hate is more useful than wishes.
- "What did they weigh up besides you, including things that aren't products, like a spreadsheet or hiring someone?"
- "What do you want customers saying about you when you're not in the room?"

### 3. The market as it really is
- "When someone looks at you and *doesn't* buy, what do they do instead?" (Only where there are real lost sales to recall; pre-launch, ask about the founder's own past switches instead. Ask about each kind of buyer. Include non-brand options like a spreadsheet, a free plugin, doing nothing, or hiring a person.)
- "What does everyone in this category compete on, the stuff every competitor's homepage says?"
- "What would you *never* associate with this category? Why not?" (This is the unexpected-value probe.)
- "Who's the big, established player now? Who were the challengers ten years ago, and are they establishment now?"
- "How much do buyers care about this category? Do they research it for weeks, or grab something in thirty seconds?" (toilet paper rule)

### 4. Weaknesses, sacrifices, repelling
- "Where do competitors genuinely beat you? Be honest."
- "What if you got *worse* at that on purpose? What would it free you to do?" (contrarian value)
- "Which customers would you happily hand to a competitor?"
- "Who should *hate* this? Who should it turn away?" (opposites game)
- "What are you not willing to give up, no matter what?"

### 5. Ambition and constraints (kept separate from the strategy)
- "What does winning look like in two or three years? Give me a number if you have one." Record it as a **goal**, not a strategy.
- "Who has the time to actually make a change? Where's the spare capacity?" (Execution killer #1: no slack.)
- "Anything off the table: money, people, brand, partners?"

### 5b. Room to move (real past only)
The Moves need to know what the business could stretch to. Ask about what has actually happened, never "would you ever…".
- "What have people asked for that you haven't built? Who asked?"
- "Who has turned up using it that you didn't expect, or used it for something you didn't plan?"
- "Is there a part of it people get excited about that you treat as a side feature?"
- "What would you be willing to build, change or stop doing if the gap were big enough? What's off limits no matter what?"
- "What do you and your team know or have that most companies in this space don't?" (strengths that would carry over)

Record the answers as facts for the gap hunt. Don't ask them to pick a direction; finding the Moves is the skill's job.

### 6. Reacting to rough angles
Once hunches form, play back two or three of them, plus one deliberately provocative contrarian one. Frame each as **what changes for whoever decides to buy** (a team leader for business software, the shopper for a consumer product, the member for a community), in plain words, not as a feature and not as "the only ___" (that phrasing is an internal test; and it lands badly said aloud). Ask:
- "What's wrong with these?"
- "Which one scares you? Why?"

The reactions, especially strong dislike, are some of the best evidence you'll get. A strong "no" often points to a real sacrifice, or to a self-loathing blind spot.

These are probes, not a menu. Never ask which one to build; choosing directions is the skill's job. Anything the founder calls "fine", "obvious" or "not compelling" goes in the Taste log as **the floor**: a strategy that only restates it fails power judging (Grader, Gate 1).

## Bringing research in

- When a competitor, alternative, nearby category or reference brand comes up, send a targeted lookup (Research Guide) and carry on with the interview.
- When findings come back, turn them into a question, not a lecture. "Fluent's homepage leads with speed, Gravity's with developers, WPForms' with beginners. Which of those, if any, is your ground?"
- If research contradicts something the founder said, ask them about it directly and record both their answer and the evidence.

## Pre-launch ideas

When the business doesn't exist yet, there's no actual path to read:
- Shift stage 2 to the founder themselves: "What do you know about this market that most people don't? Why are *you* the one to build it?"
- Lean harder on stage 3 and on research.
- In the report, call the result a **prototype strategy** to be rewritten after real time in the market (Toolkit §1, p.24).
- Ask early whether it's a familiar category done differently, or something new that has to be anchored to a category people already know (Toolkit §4).

## When to stop

You're ready when you can write down all of these:
- The real alternatives people choose instead
- The category norms
- Two or three real tendencies of the business, backed by evidence or the founder's words
- What the founder will and won't give up
- How much buyers care about the category

Then sum up what you heard in about five lines and ask "build it?". If the founder says "build it" earlier, build with what you have, and list the gaps in the report's open-questions section.
