# No Bullshit Strategy: the toolkit

## 1. The three kinds of bullshit strategy (pp.13–17)

1. **Goals as strategy.** "Become number one", "double turnover", "revolutionise the X industry". These raise the question *how?* instead of answering it.
2. **Generic strategies.** They work, but every brand in the category could say the same thing ("our chocolate tastes good"). Founders talk themselves into believing that's unique.
3. **Fluffy, unactionable strategies.** Corporate filler like "leverage synergies" or "inspire the incredible". They leave you asking "OK, what do I *do* with that?"

> A strategy's only job is to provoke specific action. A wrong-but-actionable strategy beats a right-but-unactionable one (p.17).

The model strategy is Starbucks' "Third Space": somewhere between work and home. It is simple enough to explain in a pub, and it shaped everything from power outlets to letting people stay for hours (pp.19–20).

The book is many small lenses, not one big framework. Forcing every business through one model ("find your why") works about 20% of the time (pp.21–23). For a business that hasn't launched yet, expect to re-strategise once it has been in the market a while (p.24).

## 2. What strategy is (pp.26–50)

- **Textbook definition:** "a plan to achieve a goal" (p.27). It's true, but far too loose to be useful (p.28–29).
- **The book's definition: the unique value a business provides to the market** (p.30). A business is a system that delivers value and gets value back.
- **Value-based vs non-value strategies:** Mars uses cheap manufacturing to buy shelf space (non-value). IKEA uses flat-pack savings to fund design for everyone (value). Every business must deliver value; few have non-value levers (pp.31–33).
- **Value is the benefit, not the product.** "We make cars" is the means, not the value (p.34). Draw it as Business → unique value offering (the strategy) → Products 1, 2, 3 (p.36). Confusing the company with its product blocks differentiation and long-term adaptation. Nike, Red Bull and Harley haven't changed strategy in 40 years, only how they deliver it (p.37).
- **Unique means the customer can't get it anywhere else** (p.38). "Impeccable service", "reliability" and "winning cases" are category-generic. **The market doesn't reward better; it rewards different** (p.39).
- **"Only is better than best"** (Srinivas Rao, p.40). Bad: "we are the best X". Good: "we are the only X who do Y" (p.41). "The only hotel with *truly excellent* service" is a "better" strategy in disguise.
- **Warning: unique value nobody wants** (p.42). A USP (a product quirk such as a three-slice toaster) is not a value offering for the whole business. The test is that it's unique, wanted by a lot of people, and big enough to animate an entire brand (p.44). You find that through lateral thinking, not by asking customers what they want.
- **The Strategic Hierarchy** (p.46):
  - **Strategy:** the unique value
  - **Delivery:** how products and behaviours deliver it
  - **Branding:** how it's communicated

  Each layer flows from the one above. Apple example: friendly, accessible computers (strategy) → toy-like colourful iMac with a pointless handle (delivery) → "Think Different" (branding) (pp.47–49).
- **The full standard** (p.50): compelling value · that lots of people want · that they can't get elsewhere · that your products deliver well · that your brand communicates memorably.
- *Strategy is an alternative to effort* (p.45).

## 3. Reading the market (pp.51–62)

- Two things bound a strategy: **market conditions** and **the nature of your business**. Strategy is where they overlap (Venn diagram, p.52).
- **The market is space.** Brands that offer the same value sit on top of each other and fight. A brand that sits alone has a peaceful time (p.53).
- **Non-competition mindset.** The aim is no direct competitors, not being "more competitive". Competing makes businesses copy each other's moves until they become interchangeable (pp.54–55).
- **Clustering / commodification** (p.56–57): everyone offers the same value, so the only levers left are cost-cutting and spend, which kills margin. Example: US airlines in the 1990s all fought for business travellers, and every major carrier went bust by the mid-2000s.
- **Declustering** (p.58): let competitors keep fighting while you leave. Southwest dropped business travellers entirely, and with them business class, lounges and the popular routes (p.59).
- **Strategy is deciding what not to do.** Treat competitors as things to move around and complement, not things to beat (p.60).
- **The market as an ecosystem.** Each brand does a separate, complementary job. The "perfect market" is Jeep, Ferrari, Rolls-Royce and Skoda, with no overlap (pp.60–62).

## 4. Reading your company (pp.63–80)

- **The target position is "familiar but different"** (p.68). There are two ways in:
  - **Weirding the normal** (most businesses): a familiar category, done differently (Starbucks, IKEA, Southwest). You start inside the cluster and push out. The reaction you want: *"I thought I knew X, but wow, this is totally different"* (pp.64–65).
  - **Normalising the weird:** a genuinely new product that confuses people. Pull it towards an existing category it can borrow meaning from. Juice shots got nowhere framed as juice; framed as sports drinks and supplements, they became the fresh, natural alternative (pp.66–70).
- **The strategy is already in the business.** Look for the unique traits that could open up market space if you push them hard enough. Founders are rarely aware of what those traits are (p.71).
- **The Dolly Principle:** *"Find out who you are and do it on purpose"* (p.72). The market reinterprets what you launched. Compare the imagined path with the actual path (p.73). Signals worth reading: the product that failed and the one that took off, the audience that ignored you and the one that embraced you, the channel that over-performs.
- **Experimentation, then acceleration.** Run the unstrategic prototype, watch where it drifts, then write the strategy and growth accelerates. Facebook began as hot-or-not (pp.74–75).
- **You can't transform a business, only intensify where it already leans.** Trying to pull it elsewhere is like a man pulling an elephant (pp.76–77). Lean into the tendency (diagram, p.77). Exception: a business that is failing in every direction may have nothing worth building on (p.78).
- **The self-loathing business.** Management gets bored of the profitable, unsexy position and tries to become something "groovier". It wastes millions and alienates its customers (pp.78–79). No position is desirable or undesirable in itself; execution and commitment make it exciting (Ryanair). Dull brands are timid and spread across several positions (p.80).

## 5. Three discovery methods (pp.80–115)

The evidence to use is mostly observation, not data (p.82): actions that worked surprisingly well, actions that failed, the product's real strengths and weaknesses against competitors, a helicopter view of the market, adjacent markets, and category norms. "You can never research your way to a strategy."

### Method 1: Context shift (pp.83–93). Start here, because it's the fastest.
Frame the same product against a different category or set of competitors. Value depends entirely on context.
- The cereal bar had become one of the least healthy health bars. Moved into confectionery, it became "the unhealthy person's health food", its sugar became a strength, and it won corner shops and vending machines without changing the product (pp.85–87).
- **The true competitor** is anything people might buy *instead of you*. Harley's true competitor is a conservatory. Southwest's true competitor was buses. A bar's is staying home with the TV. Starbucks' was the office (pp.87–91).
- **How to find it:** list the different reasons people buy you, list their other options for each reason, and judge your advantage over each option (p.90).
- Lean towards the surprising category without ever fully joining it, and you land in empty space (diagram, p.90). "Category" is arbitrary. Strong brands get bought as themselves, not chosen from a category (Tesla, Patagonia, Harley) (pp.91–92).

### Method 2: Unexpected value (pp.93–103). The most obvious method.
Ask: *"What value would make sense in this category, but nobody has delivered because it seemed irrelevant?"*
- Every category has locked-in core values (detergent cleans, restaurants have good food, computers are powerful). They matter, but building on them causes clustering (pp.94–95).
- **Method** gave cleaning products beautiful packaging, so they came out from under the sink, which also reinforced its eco credentials. **Xero** took "beautiful business" into accounting software (pp.95–98).
- The value has to be unexpected but not random. It should reinforce something the category already cares about. "Novel, not nonsensical" (pp.98–99).
- **Two questions:** *What is our category not?* Then *Why?* Is the gap there for a reason, or is it a failure of imagination? (p.99). Then check the fit: is your business the natural one to make this move? (p.100)
- **Look inside first:** *What do we already offer, even by accident, that's unexpected or weird for the category?* (p.100). Airbnb wasn't really about price or socialising. It was about belonging and "not being a tourist", which became "Belong Anywhere" (pp.101–103). The three questions: *What are we doing that's unexpected AND working? How do we capture it? How do we double down?*

### Method 3: Contrarian value (pp.104–115). The best method, and the hardest.
*"Everyone in this category thinks X, but we think Y."* You stop offering something the category values, so you can offer something new that's incompatible with it.
- **Nintendo Switch** deliberately underperformed on power, which made the hybrid handheld possible, and it became the bestselling console (pp.105–106). **Southwest** gave up business travellers (pp.106–107).
- **It's nearly impossible to copy**, because a competitor would have to undermine its own offer to follow (p.106).
- **Hard part 1: give up market share on purpose.** Say plainly that some customers would be better off with competitors, and respect what those competitors are good at (p.108). Nokia should have doubled down on tough, cheap, everyman phones as the counterpoint to the iPhone. Instead it copied the iPhone and died. Apple took about 15% market share but 79% of industry profit (pp.109–111). The result is a walled garden in the middle of a battlefield.
- **Hard part 2: cultivate your weaknesses.** Find where you underperform, then ask what would happen if you underperformed even more, on purpose (pp.112–113). The Savannah Bananas spent less on baseball and more on fan experience, and their baseball improved too (pp.113–114).
- **Formula** (p.115): reject something the category values → underperform at, neglect or attack it → introduce new value that's incompatible with the old.

## 6. Working it through: the humble chat (pp.115–121)

The number-one tool is an informal, one-to-one, open-ended chat, not workshops or analysis. Chats are **opinion-based** (insight lives in how facts interact), **candid** (off the record), **pressured** (two people, nowhere to hide, the silence has to be filled) and **roomy** (tangents and surprise). The author's whole method: internalise the principles, then talk to people who know the topic. That's how 75%+ of their strategies are born.

## 7. The tests: turning an idea into a strategy (pp.122–161)

A useful strategy aligns the whole business, sparks ideas immediately, and is understood by everyone. *Bad thinking crafted into a usable strategy beats good thinking that isn't* (p.123). Remove the parts that don't look like David (p.124).

1. **The first goal: make you different from yourself** (pp.124–134). Ask: *Do we have to change in an obvious way to execute this?* Anything short of "yes, duh" means it isn't a strategy.
   - *Error 1, vague or pretty strategies.* "Inspire the incredible in the everyday" demands nothing. IKEA's clunky version, "designer interiors accessible to everyone regardless of wealth or background", immediately demands homewares, design investment, low cost through flat-pack and out-of-town stores, wide availability, and designs for small spaces, kids and disability. Keep it clunky. A tagline ("The wonderful everyday") comes later (pp.127–130).
   - *Error 2, "we already do this".* Either you are, and it's a weak strategy, or you're rationalising. Test: *Would an external observer, like a customer, agree?* A value strategy should be visible from outside. Tesla doesn't need to run ads explaining itself. If you have to argue that you're on-strategy, you've already lost. Changes should be "big, binary, and in your face" (pp.130–134).
2. **The subjectivity test** (pp.134–139). *Remove every subjective word (good, great, incredible, world-class, best, smart) and see what's left.* Subjective words describe the reaction you want, not what you'll do (the Ritz and a B&B both offer "outstanding service"). They also signal a "better" strategy. Judgements belong to the market. Yoghurt example: "best-tasting" fails; "replicate the flavours and codes of ice cream in yoghurt" is an actual strategy.
3. **The opposites game** (pp.139–142). *Would the opposite of this strategy also make sense?* Low-cost vs premium: yes. A law firm that "wins cases" or a delivery firm that's "punctual": no. Only strategies a competitor might sensibly choose not to follow are legitimate. Variant: *Who are we trying to repel?* If nobody would reject it, it's not a strategy (Tesla's luxury performance EVs repelled economy EV buyers).
4. **The how cascade** (pp.142–149). This catches the hidden "goal/strategy", like "number-one dog food for pensioners". Keep asking *how?* until the answer is a solution, not an aspiration: double revenue → how? → number one for pensioners → how? → tailor to lap dogs, which they disproportionately own → *"Ah, OK, got it."* Strategy is the first layer that offers a solution, before you get into execution. Diagram: ambitions at the top, actions at the bottom, strategy as the dashed line in between (p.147). A strategy is a service to people, not a display of ambition.
5. **The toilet paper rule** (pp.149–155). Match the sophistication of the strategy to how much people care about the category. Andrex: softness, sold by puppies. Lululemon and Patagonia can afford rich brand worlds. The more boring the category, the shallower the brand needs to be (diagram, p.152). Nobody wants a lecture from their toothpaste about the rainforest. Business books mislead low-interest brands (Cirque du Soleil in *Blue Ocean Strategy*). *If people don't care about your category, don't try to make them.* Aim for short, sharp, black-and-white leverage. Respect the customer's time and biases.
6. **The challenger trap** (pp.156–161). Yesterday's challengers (Ben & Jerry's, Patagonia, The Body Shop, Innocent) are today's status quo. Copying them means fighting the status quo with the status quo. A challenger juice brand today would be *against* Innocent. Health, simplicity, environment and activism are heavily mined, so treat them as hygiene factors: "Yes, of course we're responsible, but what we really want to talk about is X." "We're *actually* ethical" is a "better" strategy. Challenge the status quo as it is today, not an imaginary one.

## 8. Writing it up (pp.162–182)

- **Ditch the deck.** Explain it the way you would to someone in a bar, in plain sentences, no diagram, no jargon. Write one page in Word, not PowerPoint, with a few paragraphs of argument building to a punchline: "therefore we are going to do X" (pp.163–165).
- **Why prose works** (pp.165–170):
  1. It makes a case, like a lawyer's closing argument, and deals with obvious objections along the way.
  2. People remember the story and retell it in their own words. Memorising it word for word means they don't understand it.
  3. It exposes bad thinking. If the strategy falls apart while you write it, that's good.

  Can't write? Record yourself explaining it and write that up. Writing is a service, not a performance.
- **Four sections** (pp.171–181). The core argument runs under a page; the whole document runs 1–3 pages.
  1. **Background:** what the business is and the relevant market conditions, written for someone who has never heard of either.
  2. **Strategy argument:** the status quo → why it falls short → what we're prepared to do differently.
  3. **Strategy summary:** one clunky, explicit sentence, plus an optional shorthand name for people who already get it (Tesla: *"establish electric cars as symbols of luxury and performance, rather than frugality and compromise"*, nicknamed "the trickle-down strategy").
  4. **Delivery:** the 2–3 binary actions that separate doing the strategy from not doing it, grouped by area (Product / Distribution / Branding and design...).

  The full Tesla example is on pp.173–181.
- **The finished shape** (p.182): here's the situation → our reading of it → our idea → how we deliver it → how we package it.

## 9. Execution (pp.183–233)

**The action is everything** (p.183). In the author's experience about 40% of brands execute decently and 20–30% execute terribly. Why (pp.184–186):
1. Execution is on top of the day job, so you need slack.
2. It needs weeks or months to sink in before it runs naturally.
3. Rationalisation: "we have a strategy, therefore we are strategic."

The fix is an **outside observer** who holds you accountable.

- **Minimum viable strategy (MVS)** (pp.187–193). *What's the bare minimum we need to do to genuinely be following this strategy?* Usually: small tweaks to existing products, planning for a new product, and a rebrand. Do it in 6–12 months. Second version of the question: *What's the minimum for customers to understand our value without us telling them?* Marketing is the cherry on top, never the substitute. A strong strategy should produce a flood of ideas; the MVS is the 2–3 foundational ones chosen from that flood. Order matters: Tesla launched the sports car before adding in-car games. **Business B (90% generic, 10% different) is the sweet spot**; C (30/70) is too strange. Work "big to small".
- **Cross-category copying** (pp.193–202). The strategy usually forces a rebrand: keep the brand's essence, but intensify it. The strategy document is the agency brief. *Copy the brand codes of businesses with similar value in completely different categories.* Domino's looks like a delivery company (FedEx/UPS), not a pizza brand. Tesla looks like Apple. Vitaminwater looks like pill bottles. The iMac looked like a toy or sweets. Lush stores look like a fruit-and-veg market. This gives you "familiar but different". Exception: "normalising the weird" brands *should* copy their target category's codes (juice shots looked like Powerade). The market is deeply conservative, so don't drift into freakish territory.
- **The creative canvas** (pp.202–214). Strategy is binary (on or off), but it's also a spectrum of how far you take it. Iconic brands over-deliver: Southwest's jokey cabin crew, Patagonia's free repairs forever and boxless underwear, Red Bull turning into a media company. Widen the canvas from ads and visual identity to the *whole business*. For a gym that means space design, equipment, staff profile, staff behaviour, amenities, and even whether to have locker rooms (Curves dropped them). "Make interesting companies, not interesting advertising." Talkable businesses become their own ads. **The packaged-goods (CPG) dilemma:** when the only moving part is the product, execute the MVS and consider format and packaging (Tetley's round bags), process innovation, ritual innovation (Corona's lime, Jägerbombs), or brand proxies (Red Bull's events).
- **The correct use of a "why"** (pp.214–222). A why (or mission) is a leadership and motivation tool, not strategy. *Strategy = what we'll do and why it will work. Why = why it would be good if we succeeded.* Sinek's Apple why is strategically empty: cheese computers would also "think different". A why connects frontline staff to the strategy. Three kinds:
  1. **Consumer benefit:** often too humble to inspire.
  2. **Category change:** good for low-interest categories.
  3. **Social good:** don't tip into parody (Starbucks' "inspire and nurture the human spirit").

  A why doesn't have to be unique. Quantify it, even with rough numbers ("transition 100 million households by 2040").
- **Keep moving to stand still** (pp.222–233). The strategy should last more or less forever; only execution changes. **If you stand still and the market doesn't, your strategy changes without you noticing**: post-Jobs Apple drifted from accessible to luxury, and Innocent drifted from cutting-edge to mid-market. Nike has held "the struggle" for about 50 years by re-executing it for each era, from training in the rain to athletes' social justice movements. **Multi-layer pacing** (pp.229–230): Strategy (flat line) → Brand (slow wave) → Product → Operations → Marketing (fast squiggle). A position stays contestable until long consistency makes it yours. After that, a competitor could even do it better and it wouldn't matter. Novelty for its own sake is vanity.
