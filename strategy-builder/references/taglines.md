# Taglines

A tagline is the strategy's **external rallying cry**. It's the line customers repeat. It comes *after* the strategy and never replaces it (Toolkit §8). The plain strategy statement says what the business does. The tagline makes people feel it.

Source: @brandbosshq (Brand Boss), "mini master class on how to do taglines the right way" (TikTok video 7693671495536610573, https://www.tiktok.com/@brandbosshq/video/7693671495536610573, transcribed 2026-10-27).

## The four types

Use the four types as **lenses for writing candidates**, not as quotas for the page. Write candidates in every type; show only the three best, whatever their types. (Filling one slot per type produced filler: a 2026-10-30 run showed four lines per strategy.)

1. **Directive.** It gives people something to go and do.
   - Nike: "Just do it." The Army: "Be all you can be." Sprite: "Obey your thirst."
2. **Big experience promise.** It puts a big promise in front of people, so they think "ooh, I really want that."
   - Disney: "The happiest place on Earth." BMW: "The ultimate driving machine."
3. **Reason to believe.** A direct, concrete proof of what you get.
   - M&M's: "Melts in your mouth, not in your hands." Bounty: "The quicker picker-upper." FedEx: "When it absolutely, positively has to be there overnight."
4. **Identity.** It's wrapped around who the customer is, or who they want to become.
   - L'Oréal: "Because you're worth it." Apple: "Think different." Adidas: "Impossible is nothing."

## The two traps (automatic fail)

1. **Safe boardroom lines that mean nothing.** These are lines everyone approves in a meeting and nobody remembers, such as a hospital saying "we're in love with life". If any competitor could run it, it fails.
2. **Lines about the company instead of the customer.** "We're…", "Our…", "The leading…". The customer has to be the subject, or at least the one who benefits.

## Lessons from real sessions

- **It must mean something on its own.** Read cold, a stranger can say what they get. A two-line riddle that only makes sense once you know the product fails this.
- **It must pull, not just describe.** A list of what's inside ("Invoices, receipts and payroll in one app") is a **subline**, not a tagline. It reads as sterile.
- **Read it as the skeptical buyer.** If the buyer could hear "lazy", "surveillance", "chaos" or "a cost", it fails. A line promising staff "no more timesheets" can read as "no more accountability" to the boss who pays; a line about seeing every customer's activity can read as spying.
- **What works:** a directive with a point of view ("Just do it"), paired with a subline that says what's inside.

## What their other videos add (2026-10-27)

From 20 more tagline videos (see [Brand Boss Library](brand-boss-library.md) §5.1):
- **Short.** Five words or fewer, three for bonus points. A longer pick has to pull harder than every shorter option to win.
- **The promise against the problem.** Write the tagline last, as the short form of the problem and the promise. If it isn't that, it's decoration.
- **Not a description.** "If your tagline is a description of you, rethink it." Description belongs in the subline.
- **Internal vs external.** A purpose line rallies the team ("recapture the magic of childhood"). The tagline rallies customers ("The happiest place on earth"). Translate one into the other; never broadcast the internal one. The purpose line goes in "Why it matters".
- **Generic to differentiated.** "We help companies grow" becomes "We help underdog founders punch above their weight class". If it could be anyone's, sharpen it until it couldn't.
- **Directive lines work best on a human truth.** "Just do it" came from a real person's last words, not a brainstorm.

## The ban list (automatic fail)

Each of these has reached the founder and failed.
- **Two claims stapled together.** "Fast setup. Happy clients." Two benefits in one line is a list, not a promise. One idea per line.
- **A feature description.** If it names what the product does ("Time sheets in one click"), it's a subline.
- **Formula lines.** "X means X", "X stays X", "Less X, more Y", "Your X, your way", "X, without the Y". They sound like taglines and say nothing.
- **Wordplay that only works written down.** Puns, rhymes, hidden meanings, invented spellings.
- **A line nobody would say out loud.** If a buyer wouldn't repeat it to a colleague, it's copy, not a tagline.
- **HR-poster or SaaS-homepage voice.** "Empower your team", "Work, together", "Where great work happens".
- **The two traps above,** and anything a skeptical buyer could hear as lazy, as surveillance or as a cost.

## The benchmarks

The bar is the lines in the four types above: "Just do it", "Think different", "Belong anywhere", "The ultimate driving machine", "When it absolutely, positively has to be there overnight", "Melts in your mouth, not in your hands". Each is one idea, says something about the customer or what they get, and comes straight from the strategy. Airbnb's line is the short form of its bet (belonging, not tourism), and FedEx's is the short form of its promise.

## How the skill uses this

1. **Write taglines last,** after the strategy's core has cleared power judging. A tagline can't rescue a weak strategy; it only exposes one.
2. **Write 20–30 candidates privately,** across all four types. Each is the short form of the bet against the assumption the strategy fights. Start from the buyer's own words and the human truth under the pain, not from the feature list.
3. **A separate tagline judge picks.** Use a fresh agent that sees only the bet sentence, the buyer, the assumption and the candidate list, never the builder's notes. It:
   - strikes every line on the ban list;
   - scores the rest 1–10 against the benchmarks, using the **billboard test**: would the company pay for a billboard with only this line and the logo, and would the buyer repeat it to a colleague?
   - returns the top three with their scores and one line on each.
4. **The tab shows three lines:** the pick, paired with a descriptive subline that says what you get, and two runners-up. Each is labelled with its type and a short note on what it says read cold.
5. **The pick must score 8 or more.** If none does, write a fresh batch from a different angle (a different human truth, a different type) and judge again, up to twice. If there's still no 8, show the best three labelled "working lines, none strong yet". Don't dress up a 6.
6. The Grader's test 13 checks the three shown lines and the judge's score.
